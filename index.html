Project Overview

"How do we keep essential workers safe, connected, and effective when the world shuts down?"

This project investigates the intersection of pandemic preparedness and remote work resilience for critical infrastructure workers — the people who keep power grids running, water flowing, hospitals stocked, and supply chains intact, regardless of what emergency unfolds.

Through primary interviews, secondary research, design documentation, and a working prototype pitch, this portfolio captures a full human-centered design process from problem scoping through to proposed solution.

The work emerged from a recognition that "working from home" is not an option for large segments of the essential workforce, yet pandemic policies were often designed with knowledge workers in mind.

01

Secondary Research

⚡

Critical Infrastructure Vulnerability

Essential sectors including energy, water, transportation, healthcare, and food supply depend on a workforce that cannot simply "log in remotely." Research mapped the unique exposure risks facing these workers during pandemic surges, including proximity to colleagues, shared equipment, and high-contact public environments.

🏥

Existing Policy Gaps

Early pandemic guidance focused heavily on office-based remote transitions, leaving operational roles underserved. Research identified inconsistencies in PPE allocation, exposure notification protocols, and mental health support for shift workers who faced elevated risk with fewer flexible arrangements.

📡

Technology as Bridge

A key research thread explored how digital tools — from real-time health monitoring to remote diagnostics and asynchronous communication platforms — could extend the capabilities of on-site workers while reducing unnecessary physical presence through smarter shift design and digital oversight.

🧠

Mental Health & Burnout

Secondary sources consistently flagged psychological toll as an underappreciated dimension of pandemic resilience. Essential workers reported elevated anxiety, moral injury, and compassion fatigue. The research grounded our design in the understanding that resilience is emotional as much as operational.

🏗️

Organizational Preparedness

Organizations with pre-existing continuity plans, decentralized command structures, and trained crisis teams demonstrated dramatically better outcomes. Research surfaced best practices from industries with strong safety cultures — nuclear, aviation, oil & gas — as models transferable to other critical sectors.

🌐

Global Comparative Cases

Cross-country comparison revealed how Taiwan, South Korea, and New Zealand maintained infrastructure stability through early, coordinated action. These cases informed design principles around rapid information flow, decentralized decision authority, and trust between workers and management.

03

Pitch & Prototype

The pitch articulates our solution concept to an audience of stakeholders who need to understand both the urgency of the problem and the feasibility of the proposed approach.

Our prototype envisions a platform that empowers critical infrastructure organizations to maintain worker safety and operational continuity during public health emergencies. Rather than relying on improvised responses, the tool builds resilience ahead of time.

The design centers three principles: anticipation (systems in place before emergencies strike), transparency (workers know what's happening and why), and agency (workers have meaningful input into their conditions).

Real-Time Health Status

Workers report health status through a simple daily check-in. The system flags exposure risks and enables dynamic shift reallocation before problems escalate.

Emergency Communication Hub

A centralized channel for verified information — cutting through rumor and confusion with authoritative, role-specific updates from organizational leadership.

Continuity Planning Tools

Scenario planning modules help teams model workforce disruptions and identify single points of failure before an emergency materializes.

Wellbeing & Support Access

Integrated pathways to mental health resources, peer support networks, and financial assistance programs — reducing the stigma of asking for help during a crisis.

Problem Statement

Critical infrastructure workers are structurally excluded from the protective measures — remote work, flexible schedules, geographic distancing — that pandemic policies most commonly prescribe. When public health emergencies strike, these workers face a binary choice: continue working under elevated risk, or abandon their post and compromise the systems their communities depend on.

How might we design tools and systems that allow critical infrastructure workers to maintain operational effectiveness during public health emergencies without sacrificing their personal health, safety, and wellbeing?

The problem is not a lack of technology — it is a lack of technology designed specifically for this population, their constraints, and their needs.

Primary Persona

Marcus, 38 — Wastewater Treatment Plant Operator

Marcus has worked in public utilities for 14 years. He is considered essential by every definition: the plant cannot run without qualified operators on site, 24 hours a day. During the pandemic, Marcus worked through fear, watched colleagues fall ill, and received information about protective policies inconsistently through a patchwork of texts, union bulletins, and overheard conversations.

His core needs: reliable information he can trust, clear protocols that protect him without humiliating him, and confidence that the organization has his back when things get hard.

Design Principles

1. Dignity first. Any solution must treat workers as partners, not data points. Monitoring tools that feel surveillance-oriented will be resisted and will fail.

2. Simplicity under stress. Emergency conditions degrade cognitive capacity. Interfaces must be operable under stress, fatigue, and time pressure.

3. Built for connectivity gaps. Critical infrastructure facilities often have limited internet access. Solutions must function offline or in low-bandwidth environments.

4. Institutional trust as prerequisite. Technology alone cannot solve a trust deficit. The platform must be paired with genuine organizational commitments to worker wellbeing.

Key Constraints

The design process surfaced several non-negotiable constraints that any solution must respect:

Workers in regulated industries face strict data privacy requirements — health status data is especially sensitive. Any platform collecting wellbeing information must meet HIPAA and sector-specific compliance standards.

Shift workers and those in field roles rarely sit at a desk. The solution must be mobile-first, quick-to-use, and designed for glanced interactions rather than sustained attention.

Labor relations are a design constraint as much as a social one. Any organizational adoption requires union consultation and worker buy-in from the earliest stages.

Opportunity Areas

Information credibility: Workers reported that misinformation spread faster than official guidance during the pandemic. A verified, role-specific information layer represents a clear and immediate opportunity.

Peer connection: Isolation — even when physically co-located with colleagues — was a recurring theme. Digital tools that strengthen peer support networks can complement existing organizational structures.

Pre-emptive planning: Most preparedness work happens reactively. Tools that help organizations run through scenarios and identify gaps before emergencies occur represent high-value, underserved territory.

Interview Insights

We kept showing up because someone had to. But nobody told us what was really going on — we were just expected to be there.

Participant — Essential Services Worker

Insight: Information asymmetry between management and frontline workers created anxiety that compounded physical risk. Workers who feel informed feel safer, regardless of the actual hazard level.

The hardest part was not the virus. It was not knowing if the organization actually had a plan, or if we were just figuring it out as we went.

Participant — Logistics Operator

Insight: Perceived organizational preparedness was a stronger predictor of worker stress than actual exposure risk. Visible planning signals matter enormously to workforce confidence.

There was no space to say "I'm struggling." You just pushed through. That's the culture. But it wears you down faster than you realize.

Participant — Healthcare Support Staff

Insight: Psychological safety is a prerequisite for workers to use wellbeing tools. Platforms that require self-disclosure in a culture of stoicism will see low adoption without cultural shifts alongside technology.

My team figured out our own system — text messages, a shared spreadsheet. It worked, but it was exhausting to maintain on top of everything else.

Participant — Utility Sector Supervisor

Insight: Workers are already building informal workarounds. Good design should formalize and improve what people are already doing rather than replacing organic solutions with top-down mandates.

06

Reflections & Learnings

01

Empathy is earned, not assumed

Sitting with interview participants and genuinely suspending assumptions was the most valuable skill developed in this process. The people we spoke with had sophisticated understandings of their own situations — our job was to listen and learn, not to arrive with pre-formed solutions.

02

Constraints are the design material

The toughest constraints — offline operation, labor relations, HIPAA compliance — turned out to be the most generative. Working within real boundaries produced ideas that could actually exist in the world, rather than elegant concepts that would never survive contact with reality.

03

Teams diverge and converge

Collaborative design requires tolerance for disagreement during ideation and commitment to convergence during delivery. Learning to hold both — encouraging diverse thinking and then synthesizing into coherent direction — was a key interpersonal and professional development throughout the semester.
